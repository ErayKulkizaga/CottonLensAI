What USDA's cotton forecast misses, and how to see it earlier

USDA's monthly Crop Production report is the benchmark every cotton desk trades against. On 23 seasons that were never seen during development (1987–2011), a rule built only from USDA's own revisions was closer to the final crop than USDA in 88 of 128 comparisons (p = 0.007), and it called the direction of USDA's next revision two times in three (p = 0.012, preregistered). Since 2012 the edge has faded, and the reason is a change in what USDA gets wrong: Texas misses moved from yield to harvested area. Public crop-condition data sees that abandonment coming, and USDA's in-season area barely moves. Every test here was written down before its data was opened, and the failures are kept.

Evaluation protocol

How the years were spent

blind test (T45)developmentsealed, opened once (T39, T42)forward, locked
  • Rolling origin. For season Y every model is refit on seasons before Y. No random K-fold anywhere: time-series leakage through shuffled folds was ruled out by construction.
  • Preregistration. Pass rules were committed and pushed before the data they judged was read. Amendments made before outcomes were seen are dated in the record.
  • Two sealed holdouts, each opened once. 2022–2024 (T39) and 2025 (T42). Both failed and both failures are kept.
  • A blind era. USDA's 2000–2011 forecasts were never pulled until T45, so their errors were unseen when the test was written.
  • Multiple-comparison awareness. About 60 variants were tried in development; best single cells were treated as optimistic, never as claims.

Why USDA misses · T48

Texas: from a yield problem to a harvested-area problem

Production is harvested area × yield, so USDA's error splits exactly into an area part and a yield part (the identity holds to ±0.1% in the data). Before 2012, Texas misses were yield misses. Since 2012 they are mostly harvested-area misses: USDA's in-season area has usually been too high, i.e. abandonment was under-estimated, and it barely moves between August and December.

The preregistered test that the 2012–2025 pattern also held in 1995–2011 failed on two of four hypotheses: the pattern is a change, not a constant. USDA learns little about harvested area in-season in both periods (December area error is 79–98% of August's). The United States as a whole stays a yield story. This points the research at one question: how much planted Texas cotton will not be harvested, and when can public data see it?

A published effect, re-tested · T44–T47

Revision momentum: small, real in 2002–2025, shrinking

Studies of USDA cotton forecasts (Isengildina-Massa, MacDonald & Xie 2012) and corn and soybean forecasts (Isengildina, Irwin & Good 2013) find "smoothing": successive revisions are positively correlated, so the latest revision carries information about the remaining error. I tested whether cotton forecasts share it, with one spec, no intercept, and nothing tuned on the test years.

rs,y = log(USDAm / USDAm−1)  ·  ês,y = b · rs,y / σr  ·  forecast = USDAm · exp(ê)
b = Σ z·e / (Σ z² + λ),   z = r / σr,   σr = RMS(r),   λ = 1,   pooled over states, fitted on seasons < y

No intercept on purpose: a bias term ("USDA usually over-forecasts") is exactly what broke out of sample (see the bias flip below). The momentum rule is sign-agnostic: it follows whichever direction USDA itself just moved.

Why the obvious model failed

USDA's bias flipped, and a bias correction flipped with it

The first model to beat USDA in development (T25) simply removed USDA's average past error. USDA had over-forecast US cotton for most of 2014–2021, so the correction pushed numbers down. From 2022 USDA started under-forecasting in some years, and the same correction made it worse.

From research to a running system · ADR 0001

A point-in-time monitor that reproduces the locks

The frozen rules now run as a small, auditable pipeline (research/monitor/):

  • it reads USDA's public report text on release day, with no API key;
  • it keeps only table rows where production = area × yield;
  • it checks gates (states sum to the US, a previous report exists, condition data is at most 21 days old, revisions are in range) and falls back to USDA's own number on any failure;
  • it applies a frozen model file trained through 2025 and attaches empirical 80/95% intervals;
  • it appends each result to a hash-chained log that detects any later edit.

What I took away

Lessons for forecasting against an official benchmark

Model the benchmark, not just the crop

The durable edge came from USDA's own revision behaviour. Physical signals (NDVI, drought, abandonment) mostly duplicated what USDA's surveys already knew.

Bias terms are regime bets

A past-mean correction is a bet that the error regime persists. It won 2013–2021 and lost three of the next four years.

Data size decides architecture

With ~2,700 labelled county-years, a CNN is unstable across seeds; shallow boosting and shrinkage models beat it.

Decompose before you model

Splitting USDA's error into area and yield showed that Texas changed regime around 2012. That explains the bias flips and the shrinking momentum better than any model trained on production alone.

Try to break your own result

The cotton rule looked general. Tested unchanged on corn and soybeans under a pushed preregistration, it added nothing. That narrowed the claim before anyone else had to.

Leakage hides in timestamps

File save times, revised archives and missing publication dates each produced results that looked like wins before review.
